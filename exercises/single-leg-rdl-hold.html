Single-Leg RDL Hold

i.

How to Do It

Stand on one leg, other foot lifted just behind you. Hinge at the hip — chest drops forward, back leg lifts behind you in a straight line. Continue until torso is roughly parallel to the floor (or as deep as your hamstring tolerance allows). Back leg straight, toe pointed, hip square (don't let the back-leg hip rotate open). HOLD. Neutral spine — no rounding, no over-arching. Stand under control by driving the standing-leg heel down. Switch legs.

ii.

Why It Works

The single-leg RDL held at the bottom is the closest off-ice analogue to a fully-extended skating stride — back leg behind you, torso angled forward, force driving back into the ground. It's a hamstring isometric in the lengthened position, a glute strength drill, AND a balance/spinal-control test all in one rep. Building hamstring strength in this lengthened position is one of the most well-documented ways to reduce hamstring strains in any sport that involves sprinting or skating push-offs. For hockey, the bonus is the unilateral nature: single-leg work exposes the asymmetries every skater has and forces both sides to develop equally.

iii.

Hockey Transfer

Hamstring iso plus balance plus spinal control all at once. The closest off-ice analogue to a fully-extended stride position — back leg driving force back into the surface. Twist open at the hip and the hold's lost. The whole exercise is in keeping the hips closed.

iv.

Coaching Cues

  • "Stand on one leg. Hinge forward — chest drops"
  • "Back leg lifts behind you. Stop when your torso is roughly parallel to the floor. HOLD. Tight back leg"
  • "Neutral spine"
  • "Hips SQUARE. Switch legs."
v.

Common Mistakes

Back-leg hip rotating open — same fault as the hip airplane; hips stay SQUARE. Rounding the lower back — neutral spine; if you can't hold flat, you're hinging too deep for your current hamstring length. Standing knee locking out — keep a soft bend so the hamstring takes the load, not the joint. Catching with the back foot when balance fails — end the set; hold is over.

vi.

Progression / Regression

Progression

Weighted single-leg RDL hold — dumbbell in the opposite-side hand (forces extra anti-rotation work).

Regression

Single-leg RDL hold with light hand support on a wall or rack — same hold, less balance demand.
