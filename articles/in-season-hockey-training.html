In-Season

In-season training: stay fast until playoffs.

Most players train hard all summer, then stop the day the season starts. By February they are slower than they were in September and nobody can explain why. Here is what the research shows, the smallest dose that stops the slide, and exactly where it goes in your week.

Coach David Ciboch
M.Ed. Sport Science · Former National Team Coach
11 min read Science-based
The 30-second version
  • The season drains your legs. Studies that tested pro and college hockey players week by week found jump height and leg power dropping as the season went on, with the worst numbers late in the year, right before playoffs.
  • One session a week holds the line. Pro soccer players who kept one strength session a week held their strength and sprint speed for 12 weeks. The group that dropped to one session every two weeks lost both.
  • Keep the weight, cut everything else. To maintain, you can cut sessions, sets and time. What you cannot cut is intensity: heavy loads and full-speed sprints.
  • Timing beats volume. Heavy leg work goes at least 48 hours before your next game. The day before a game: nothing heavy, nothing new.
  • Kids are the exception. For young players the in-season risk is usually too much, not too little. Count the hours before you add anything.

Every September, players show up to the season about as fit as they will be all year. Then the off-ice work stops. There are practices, games, school, work, travel, and the gym starts to feel like something that belongs to summer. By February the first three strides are not what they were, the legs are heavy in the third period, and the groin that never bothered anyone starts to talk.

That slide is not bad luck. It is what a long season does to a body that stops getting a strength stimulus. The good news is that stopping it takes far less work than building it did. You do not need a summer program in the middle of winter. You need the right small dose, in the right place in the week.

Why players get slower during the season

Hockey looks like it should keep you fit on its own. You are on the ice four or five times a week, skating hard, sweating through your gear. But practice is not a strength stimulus, and a long season piles up fatigue faster than practice can build anything back.

The research on this is remarkably consistent. An NHL team that tested its players with countermovement jumps every week found that jump height and velocity fell across the season, with the biggest effect of fatigue in the late season. A study of an NCAA Division I team over an 18-week season found that lower-body power declined throughout the year, with the sharpest drops after the longest travel stretches. Another college team produced less power in repeated sprints after the season than before it, and the same sprints felt harder.

A 2021 systematic review that pulled together decades of season-long hockey studies came to the same place: recent research points to aerobic deconditioning and growing fatigue as the season goes on, and the authors call for more work on limiting those losses in the later part of the year.

Read that again with playoffs in mind. The point where players are at their weakest is the point where the games matter most.

The exception: young players

A study that tested elite 13-year-old players at the start and end of a season found their muscular measures and every skating test improved during the season. Growth and lots of ice time do a lot of the work at that age. For youth players the season is not something to survive, it is a big part of how they develop. More on that below.

The minimum dose that stops the slide

Here is what most players get wrong. They assume in-season training has to look like off-season training, so when the schedule fills up they drop it completely. The research says the opposite: holding on to strength takes a fraction of the work it took to build it.

The clearest study comes from professional soccer, another sport with a long season and games every week. After a 10-week preseason with two strength sessions a week, players were split into two groups. One kept one strength session a week. The other dropped to one session every two weeks. Over the first 12 weeks of the season, the once-a-week group kept their gains in leg strength and 40 m sprint time. The every-other-week group got weaker and slower.

A 2021 review on the smallest dose of training needed to keep fitness lands in the same place. In younger people, strength and muscle size can be held for months with as little as one session a week and one set per exercise, as long as the load stays heavy. The authors' main conclusion is that intensity is the variable that matters. You can cut how often and how much. You cannot cut how hard.

To hold your strength in-season, cut the sessions, cut the sets, cut the time. Never cut the weight.

For a hockey player that means a short session: a few heavy sets, a few fast jumps and sprints, and out. Twenty-five minutes, not ninety.

The 48-hour rule

Doing the work is half of it. Doing it at the right time is the other half. A game leaves fatigue behind, and so does heavy leg training. Stack them badly and you either play tired or train tired.

Studies that track players after matches show how long that fatigue hangs around. In one recent soccer study, jump height was still down 24 and 48 hours after a match, and a marker of muscle damage was still raised at 48 hours. That is why the rule we use is simple:

  • Heavy legs: at least 48 hours before your next game.
  • Short speed and jump work: up to 48 hours before, kept low volume and fully rested.
  • Upper body, core and mobility: fine closer to a game.
  • The day before a game: mobility only. Nothing heavy, nothing new.
  • The day after a game: easy movement and mobility. Your first hard session comes after that.

Why nothing new? A new exercise makes you sore even at light loads, because your muscles have not adapted to it yet. In-season, stick to movements you already know well. Save the experiments for summer.

What about training on game day?

Some pro teams run a short "primer" on the morning of a game: a few jumps, or two or three fairly heavy reps. A 2019 review found that low-volume, high-intensity strength work can improve performance for up to 48 hours afterwards, but responses vary a lot between athletes. If you want to try it, keep it tiny and test it in a game that does not matter. Your pre-game warm-up is still the main event, and we break that down in the perfect hockey warm-up.

Your week, built around your games

Most players fit one of three schedules. Find yours and slot the sessions in exactly like this. Practices stay where your team puts them. If a practice lands on a training day, do the off-ice session after the ice, not before.

Short on time in a crazy week? Keep the strength session and drop the speed session. The strength session alone is what the research says holds your gains. Playing more than three times a week, like junior players or adults in two leagues? Drop to one 20-minute strength session on your freest day and keep the groin work. That is it.

The 25-minute in-season session

This is the main session: a little power first, then strength, in the order your nervous system wants it. It works in a gym, or at home with a pair of dumbbells. Every exercise links to a demo video.

The three rules that make it work

  1. Leave two reps in the tank on every strength set. Heavy, but never a grind. You are maintaining, not testing.
  2. Jumps and sprints only count when they are fast. The moment they slow down, the set is over.
  3. Sore the next day? Cut a set next time, never the weight. Intensity is the thing you are protecting.

Do you still need conditioning?

If you play big minutes, mostly no. Games and hard practices are your conditioning, and extra intervals on top just add fatigue you then have to recover from.

If you do not play big minutes, the answer changes. The season review above found aerobic fitness tends to slide over the year in college players, and a player on the fourth line, the third pair, the backup goalie spot or coming back from an injury gets less of the conditioning that games give everyone else. Add one short piece a week: 6 to 8 rounds of 30 seconds hard and 90 seconds easy on a bike, on a day at least 48 hours from your next game.

The one exercise you never drop

Groin strains are one of hockey's signature injuries, and they mostly happen in games. The fix is well studied. On one NHL team, players with weak adductors did a strengthening program, and adductor strains fell from 3.2 to 0.71 per 1,000 player-games over the next two seasons. In soccer, a program built on a single exercise, the Copenhagen adduction, done three times a week in the preseason and once a week during the season, cut the risk of groin problems by 41%.

Once a week is the in-season dose, and it takes two minutes. Keep the Copenhagen Plank in your main session and do not drop it when the schedule gets busy. That is exactly when you need it. What changes by age

Youth · 9 to 12

Keep off-ice play-like: two short sessions of 15 to 20 minutes with jumps and landings, bodyweight strength, sprints and games. Watch the total load first. Kids who spend more than twice as much time in organized sport as in free play get hurt more often, and the lead researcher's advice is simple: no more hours of organized sport a week than the child's age in years.

Teens · 13 to 18

The group that gains most from in-season strength work. One to two sessions a week with real loads and clean technique. During a growth spurt, if knees or heels hurt when jumping, cut the jump volume and get it checked by a physio rather than pushing through.

Adults · 19+

Usually one game a week, often late at night, on top of work. Two sessions is ideal: one strength, one speed and mobility. Respect the 48-hour rule even more as you get older, and note that the minimal-dose review found older adults may need two sessions a week and two to three sets to keep their muscle.

For parents

Before you add a single off-ice session in the middle of the season, add up the week: practices, games, skills sessions, private lessons, other sports. A 10-year-old with four practices, two games and a skills class is already close to ten hours. In that week, the best "training" you can give them is a free afternoon to play.

How to know you are doing too much

Pro teams track fatigue with weekly jump tests. That is exactly how the studies above caught the season's slide. You can run a simple version with a tape measure.

The broad jump check

Once a week, on the same day, at the end of your warm-up, do three broad jumps and write down your best. If your best is clearly down two weeks in a row, take a lighter week: same weights, fewer sets, more sleep.

How you feel is data too. In the college study above, a simple fatigue questionnaire tracked closely with jump power. Watch for these: legs heavy from the first shift, sleeping worse, a nagging groin or hip, dreading sessions you normally like. Two or more of those for a week means back off for a week. Quick answers

Should hockey players lift weights during the season?

Yes. One or two short, heavy sessions a week keep the strength and speed you built in the off-season. Players who stop completely tend to be at their weakest late in the season, right when the games matter most.

How many times a week should I train off-ice during the season?

One strength session a week is the minimum that holds your gains. Two, one strength and one speed and power, is ideal for teens and adults. Young players do best with two short, play-like sessions.

Can I lift the day before a game?

Not heavy legs. Keep heavy lower-body work at least 48 hours before a game. The day before, do mobility only.

Can I work out on game day?

A tiny primer in the morning, a few jumps or two or three fairly heavy reps, can help some players, but responses vary. Test it in a game that does not matter. Never do a full session on game day.

Do I need cardio during the season?

Not if you play big minutes. If you play little or are coming back from an injury, add one short interval session a week, at least 48 hours away from your next game.

Will I lose my summer gains during the season?

Not if you keep training heavy once a week. In professional soccer players, one session a week held strength and sprint speed for 12 weeks. One session every two weeks did not.

What should I do during a tournament weekend?

No training during the tournament. Do your strength session early in the week, recover afterwards, and use 10 minutes of mobility between games.

The bottom line

The season does not have to cost you your summer. Keep one heavy session a week, put it at least 48 hours away from your next game, add a short speed session when the schedule allows, and never drop the groin work. Twenty-five minutes a week is the difference between the player who fades in February and the one who is still flying in the playoffs.
